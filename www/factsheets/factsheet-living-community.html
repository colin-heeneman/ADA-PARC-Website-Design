Living in the Community
Among People with Disabilities

Printed by ADA-PARC, September 2025

ada

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) rests on the principle that people with disabilities have the right to live in the community, with the supports they need, rather than in an institution. The U.S. Supreme Court affirmed that right in the landmark Olmstead decision in 1999, holding that unnecessary institutionalization is a form of discrimination.1

This fact sheet shows how many people with disabilities are living in the community in each state. Community living covers two situations. Most people live at home, alone or with family. A smaller number live in non-institutional group quarters, a Census Bureau category for group settings that are not institutions, such as group homes, shelters and college dormitories.2

Nationally, 95.5 percent of people with disabilities live in the community, compared with 99.5 percent of people without disabilities. The maps and tables below show how much that figure varies from state to state.

Higher percentages mean more people with disabilities are living in the community. The residence categories on this sheet and on its companion, Living in Institution, are measured against the same population and add to 100 percent. Every percentage point not living in the community is a percentage point living in an institution.

The map below combines both community settings, people living at home and people living in non-institutional group quarters, into a single measure. States shaded darker have a larger share of their disabled residents living in the community.

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the most favorable outcomes (Excellent), here the highest share of people with disabilities living in the community. Lighter gold marks the least favorable outcomes (Poor).

Living at Home

Living at home means living in a house or apartment, alone, with family or with roommates, rather than in any kind of group setting. This is by far the most common living arrangement for people with disabilities, and it is the measure most directly tied to the independent living the ADA envisions.

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the highest share of people with disabilities living at home. Lighter gold marks the lowest.

Non-Institutional Group Quarters

Non-institutional group quarters is a Census Bureau category covering group living arrangements that are not institutions. It includes group homes and supported living for people with disabilities, but also emergency shelters, transitional housing and college dormitories.

Because the category mixes supported community living with housing instability, a higher percentage is not straightforwardly better or worse. This map is therefore shaded by how large the share is rather than by performance, and its groups are labeled by size rather than as Excellent or Poor. Read it alongside the other two measures rather than on its own.
