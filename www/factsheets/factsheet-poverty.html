Poverty

Work & Economic

Population covered: People of all ages for whom poverty status is determined The Census Bureau does not assess poverty status for everyone. People living in group quarters, including nursing homes, correctional facilities and college dormitories, and unrelated individuals under 15, are outside every figure on this sheet. Institutional residence is covered on the Living in Institution sheet.

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) rests on the idea that people with disabilities should be assured equal opportunity and economic self-sufficiency.1 More than three decades on, a person with a disability is still close to twice as likely to be living below the poverty line.

Nationally, 20.4 percent of people with disabilities live below the poverty line, against 11.3 percent of people without disabilities. That is 8.9 million people. The gap holds at every age, and it is widest during working life.2

Poverty and disability compound each other. Disability raises the cost of an ordinary life, through medical care, personal assistance, accessible housing and transport, while narrowing access to the work that would pay for it. That shows up in who is poor. People with disabilities are 13.4 percent of the people counted here, but 21.9 percent of everyone living below the poverty line.

This sheet reports the share of people living below the poverty line, first for everyone and then for three age groups. Lower percentages are better. All four measures are drawn from the same population, the people the Census Bureau assesses for poverty, so the three age groups add to the all-ages figure exactly.2

All Ages

The two maps below are the headline measure, the share of people of any age living below the poverty line, shown for people with disabilities and for people without. Each map ranks states against the other states on that map, so a shade says where a state stands among states rather than how the two groups compare. The comparison is in the ranges printed under each map. Every tier on the right sits at a lower rate than the tier of the same name on the left, and that distance is the disparity this sheet is about.

How to read the colors. Both maps share one scale and one key. A shade means the same poverty rate on either map, so the two can be read against each other directly and the difference between them is the disability gap. Darker maroon is a lower poverty rate, the more favorable outcome. Lighter gold is a higher rate. Because the scale is shared, the bands are not evenly filled on either map: the map of people with disabilities sits mostly in the higher bands and the map of people without disabilities mostly in the lower ones. That imbalance is the finding, not a ranking of the states on it. The three maps further down, which cover people with disabilities only, are each ranked against themselves and use performance tiers instead.

By Age

The three maps below cover people with disabilities only. Each is ranked against itself, in equal-count quarters, so a state’s shade says where it stands among states within that age group rather than how the age groups compare with each other. Poverty is markedly lower after 65 than before it, and the legends carry the numbers that show it. Rates for people without disabilities in each age group are in the fact sheet data rather than mapped here.

Children Under 18

Children with a disability are poorer than children without one, though this is the narrowest of the three age gaps. Child poverty is mostly a measure of the household a child lives in rather than of the child, so this map reflects family income and the added cost of raising a disabled child at the same time.

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the lowest share of disabled children below the poverty line. Lighter gold marks the highest. Each of the three age maps is ranked against itself, so the ranges on the legend differ from map to map. Read them before comparing one age group with another.

Working-Age Adults, 18 to 64

Working age is where the gap is widest. A working-age adult with a disability is 2.4 times as likely to be below the poverty line as one without. This was the only measure on this sheet until August 2026, and it is the one tied most directly to the barriers described on the companion Employment sheet.

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the lowest share of working-age disabled adults below the poverty line. Lighter gold marks the highest.

Adults 65 and Over

Poverty falls after 65 for both groups, because Social Security and Medicare reach almost everyone. The disability gap narrows but does not close. Read this map beside the other two rather than on its own, since a low rate here reflects federal income support more than anything a state has done.

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the lowest share of disabled adults 65 and over below the poverty line. Lighter gold marks the highest.
