Living in an Institution
Among People with Disabilities

Printed by ADA-PARC, September 2025

ada

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) mandates the right to live in the community with supports instead of living in a nursing home or other institution. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld this right in the landmark Olmstead decision in 1999.1

Despite that right, people with disabilities remain far more likely than other people to live in an institution. Nationally, 4.6 percent of people with disabilities live in an institutional setting, compared with 0.5 percent of people without disabilities. People with disabilities are more than eight times as likely to be institutionalized.

The Census Bureau counts three kinds of institutional settings in these data. Nursing homes and other medical facilities account for the largest share, followed by adult correctional facilities. A small remainder covers settings such as psychiatric hospitals and juvenile facilities.2

Lower percentages mean fewer people with disabilities are institutionalized and more are living in the community. Some states do considerably better than others. Where a person lives continues to shape whether they are supported at home or placed in an institution.

This map combines every institutional setting into one measure, the share of people with disabilities living in an institution rather than in the community. It is the exact complement of the combined measure on the companion sheet, Living in Community.

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the most favorable outcomes (Excellent), here the lowest rates of institutionalization and the most community living. Lighter gold marks the least favorable outcomes (Poor).

Correctional Facilities

Adult correctional facilities include prisons and prison farms, reception and diagnostic centers, and facilities for parole violators. People with disabilities are over-represented in these settings relative to their share of the general population, a pattern that reflects gaps in community mental health services, housing and support as much as anything about the criminal legal system itself.3

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the lowest share of people with disabilities living in adult correctional facilities. Lighter gold marks the highest.
