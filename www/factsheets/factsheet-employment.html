Employment
Among People with Disabilities Ages 18 to 64

Printed by ADA-PARC, September 2025

ada

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) was written to secure equal opportunity and economic self-sufficiency for people with disabilities, and Title I of the Act specifically prohibits discrimination in employment.1 More than three decades on, the gap in who holds a job remains one of the widest disparities in this data.

Nationally, 43 percent of working-age people with disabilities are employed, compared with 78 percent of working-age people without disabilities. Put another way, a person without a disability is close to twice as likely to be working.

This fact sheet shows the three employment situations the Census Bureau distinguishes for people aged 18 to 64. A person is employed, unemployed (not working but actively looking for work), or not in the labor force (neither working nor looking). Everyone is counted in exactly one of the three, so for each group the shares add to 100 percent.2 These figures were checked against the source Census table directly, and reconcile exactly.3

The largest difference is not in unemployment but in labor force participation. Just under half of working-age people with disabilities are in the labor force at all, against more than four in five people without disabilities. Barriers to getting and keeping a job, including inaccessible workplaces, transportation, benefit rules that penalize earnings, and outright discrimination, show up here rather than in the unemployment figure.

The share of working-age people with disabilities who are employed. This is the headline measure of whether a state's economy is open to disabled workers, and it varies widely, from roughly a third to well over half depending on where a person lives.

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the most favorable outcomes (Excellent), here the highest share of working-age people with disabilities in work. Lighter gold marks the least favorable outcomes (Poor).

Not in the Labor Force

The share of working-age people with disabilities who are neither working nor looking for work. This is the largest of the three categories for disabled people and the smallest for everyone else, which is the central finding of this sheet.

The category covers a range of situations, including study, caregiving, and retirement before 65, alongside people who have stopped looking after sustained difficulty finding work. It is shaded so that lower shares read as the more favorable outcome, on the reasoning that a person who wants to work should be able to. That framing will not fit every individual it counts.

How to read the colors. States are shaded by performance tier, not by raw rate. Darker maroon marks the lowest share of working-age people with disabilities outside the labor force. Lighter gold marks the highest. This category covers study, caregiving and early retirement as well as people who have stopped looking for work, so read the tier labels alongside the note above the map.

Labor Force Participation and Unemployment Rate

The maps above measure each category against everyone aged 18 to 64. The table below reports the two figures that are more commonly quoted, and they use different denominators. The labor force participation rate is the share of the age group either working or looking. The unemployment rate is the share of those in the labor force who are not working, which is the definition the Bureau of Labor Statistics uses.

The distinction matters. Nationally the unemployment rate for people with disabilities is 10.9 percent against 4.8 percent for people without, a gap of roughly two to one. The share of all disabled working-age people who are unemployed is only 5.3 percent, because so many are not in the labor force to begin with. Neither figure is wrong; they answer different questions, and quoting one while meaning the other is the most common way this data gets misread.
